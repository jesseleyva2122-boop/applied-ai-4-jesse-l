Across 20 wage settings, with five episodes per setting, nine points were Pareto maximal. Listed as wage per step (average episode profit; order completion), they were $0.25 ($15,878.95; 40.8%), $0.40 ($14,205.20; 44.8%), $0.30 ($13,928.50; 47.2%), $0.70 ($13,052.56; 50.1%), $0.60 ($10,557.26; 52.5%), $1.00 ($10,359.39; 57.3%), $1.40 ($6,058.01; 57.5%), $1.80 ($4,107.37; 65.9%), and $3.00 (-$1,006.32; 69.9%). In plain English, none of these tested choices had another choice that delivered at least as much profit and service while improving one of them. Moving toward better service along this frontier required giving up profit. A surprising result was that $3.00 achieved the highest completion percentage but reached bankruptcy in every episode. Because those episodes ended early, that percentage does not demonstrate sustainable service over a full operating period. Another warning was the demo's automatic “sweet spot” label: it selected $0.15 using profit divided by wage, even though $0.25 produced both higher profit and better completion. That ratio answers a different question than balancing the two business objectives.

I recommend testing $0.70 per step for new hires as the company's starting policy. It retained about 82.2% of the highest observed profit while raising completion by 9.3 percentage points compared with $0.25. The company would give up approximately $2,826 in average episode profit for that improvement, leaving a positive margin while serving more orders. Paying above the lowest wage options could also support employee retention, but this simulation does not measure turnover, so that benefit needs separate evidence. During a cash shortage, I would favor the profit-maximizing $0.25 configuration only if its 40.8% completion met the company's minimum service commitment. For a major customer renewal or costly service failures, I would consider $1.80, accepting lower profit for 65.9% completion. Maximizing service alone would make sense only for a short, explicitly funded emergency; I would not adopt the bankrupt $3.00 policy for normal operations. These are preliminary choices, not proven wage effects: only five episodes were tested, demand differed between settings, and some labeled wages mapped to different actual hiring wages. The recommended $0.70 setting does use $0.70 for new hires; the two initial workers remain at $0.50. Before changing payroll, I would repeat the comparison with matched demand, corrected wage mapping, and minimum profit and service targets.
